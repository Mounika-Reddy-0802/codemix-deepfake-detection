\title{Recording Domain, Not Code-Mixing: Closing the Generalisation Gap of\\
Audio Deepfake Detection on Hindi--English Telephone Speech}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A wav2vec~2.0 deepfake detector trained on English ASVspoof 2019 LA reaches 0.85\%
equal error rate (EER) on English and 48.21\% on clean Hindi--English code-mixed
speech, 58.42\% over a narrowband telephone channel. It fails by rejecting genuine
speakers. On a language-controlled set of 183 unseen speakers, holding speaker and
recording fixed, it rejects genuine English as often as code-mixed speech, and the
Code-Mixing Index does not predict its score: the gap follows the recording domain,
not the language mix. Low-rank adaptation of 1.13\% of the encoder on
speaker-disjoint in-domain speech brings channel-matched error to 2.75\% (3.27\% over
five seeds) and keeps English at 2.42\%; on 60 further unseen speakers it reaches
9.4\% on the seen cloning tool over the channel. Every result is tested, by a paired
bootstrap over speakers, against a shortcut floor of eight signal statistics fitted on
the same training data. Unseen tools remain the open problem: over the channel the
deployed model averages 35.59\% on Tortoise across five seeds, not below its floor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Zero-shot voice cloning needs seconds of a person's speech~\cite{casanova2024xtts,
betker2023tortoise}, and its criminal use is the telephone call. Detectors built on
self-supervised speech representations reach below 1\% EER on English
benchmarks~\cite{todisco2019asvspoof,tak2022wav2vec}. In India, everyday speech is
\emph{code-mixed}, Hindi and English inside one utterance, and arrives over a
narrowband channel. We ask what happens to such a detector there, why, and what it
costs to fix. Our contributions:
(i) a measured gap, characterised as a rejection of genuine speech;
(ii) a language-controlled test showing the gap follows the recording domain rather
than code-mixing;
(iii) closure by low-rank adapters~\cite{hu2022lora} on a frozen
encoder~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec}, confirmed on new speakers and five seeds;
(iv) matched shortcut floors with a paired speaker bootstrap for every claim; and
(v) an honest account of unseen-tool generalisation, including two unseen generators.

\section{Data and attacks}

Genuine code-mixed speech is MUCS 2021~\cite{diwan2021mucs} (520 speakers, 89.5~h of
lectures); English is ASVspoof 2019 LA. MUCS speakers are split into frozen,
disjoint pools before any audio is cloned: training (25), adaptation (10),
evaluation (15), and, for the extension, new test speakers (60), language control
(183) and extra adaptation speakers (227). A test fails the build if any speaker,
held-out tool or excluded file crosses a boundary.

Attacks are made from committed job tables and quality-screened: XTTS-v2 clones
for training and test, RVC~\cite{rvc2023} conversions (training uses a
speaker-held-out half), and two tools never used in training, Tortoise-TTS and
IndicF5~\cite{ai4bharat2025indicf5,chen2024f5tts}, a flow-matching Indian-language
TTS. All audio is level-normalised; the channel condition is 8~kHz G.711 $\mu$-law at
20~dB SNR~\cite{itu1988g711}. The language-controlled set tags each word of a
transcript by script (Devanagari or Latin) and takes, per control speaker, equal
numbers of all-English, all-Hindi and mixed (CMI $\geq$ 20~\cite{das2014cmi}) clips
from the same recordings: 439, 412 and 439 clips.

\section{Systems and evaluation}

\textbf{S1} fine-tunes wav2vec~2.0 base on ASVspoof 2019 LA. \textbf{S2} adds LoRA
adapters (rank 8, 1.13\% of parameters) to S1's frozen encoder and trains them on
code-mixed genuine speech and clones of 21 speakers; the \emph{deployed} S2 uses XTTS
and RVC attacks, trained over the channel. \textbf{S3} fine-tunes the whole
pretrained encoder on the same data.

A low EER on code-mixed sets can come from loudness, bandwidth or room differences.
Every detector is therefore paired with a \textbf{shortcut floor}: a logistic
regression on eight signal statistics, fitted on the detector's own training manifest
and scored on the same clips. The difference in EER gets a 95\% interval from a
bootstrap that resamples whole speakers; a result counts as learning beyond the
shortcut only if the whole interval is below zero.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The gap, and where it comes from}

S1 reaches 0.85\% EER on English, but 41.67--48.21\% on clean code-mixed sets and
44.15--58.42\% over the channel, where its AUC falls below 0.5. At a fixed threshold
it rejects 0.37\% of genuine English and 94.8\% of genuine Hindi--English speech.

Table~\ref{tab:lang} holds speaker and recording fixed. S1 rejects the control
speakers' genuine English as often as their mixed speech; within speakers, mixed
speech is rejected 9.5 points \emph{less} often than English on clean audio
[$-$15.6, $-$3.5]. Over the evaluation pool's genuine clips, S1 rejects 93.5--95.2\%
in every CMI band and the rank correlation of CMI with its score is $-$0.01. The gap
is a recording-domain gap that code-mixed telephone speech carries.

\subsection{Closing it}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the deployed S2 and S3 in their training condition. The
deployed adapter reaches 2.75\% EER over the channel on unseen speakers, 7.21 points
below its matched floor [$-$12.92, $-$1.10], and 3.27\% $\pm$ 1.17 over five training
seeds; it keeps English at 2.42\%. S3 is better on the seen tool (0.83\% clean) but
collapses to chance on English (50.90--56.42\%): each model learns its own training
domain's notion of genuine speech, and only adaptation keeps both.

the worst at 17.1\%. Both unseen tools stay near 30\%: Tortoise at 29.4\% and the
newer IndicF5 at 30.6\%, while S1 stays at 44.8--52.7\%.

On clean audio the adapter generalises to the held-out Tortoise attack (5.44\% against
a 29.49\% floor, XTTS-only training). Over the channel the deployed model averages
35.59\% $\pm$ 3.35 across five seeds against a 37.79\% floor and beats it in no seed;
randomising recording cues during training lowers this to 26.63\% $\pm$ 1.66, an
exploratory result. IndicF5 is also the most natural tool: its predicted MOS
(UTMOS~\cite{saeki2022utmos}) is 3.07 against 3.10 for the same speakers' genuine
speech, where XTTS sits at 2.66 against 3.17.

The simulated channel flatters the model. Through the reference AMR-NB mobile codec at
12.2~kbit/s, the deployed model's error on new speakers rises from 9.4\% to 23.7\% on
XTTS and to 37.0\% on Tortoise, while an adapter trained on clean audio holds at 12.6\%
on XTTS: channel training fitted one telephone chain.

\textbf{More voices.} Retrained on 248 genuine speakers instead of 21, changing only the
training manifest, the deployed recipe is better in all five seeds on new speakers
(0.91\% against 10.75\%), on Tortoise (13.59\% against 29.82\% on new speakers), on
IndicF5 (27.25\% against 35.40\%) and in false alarms (0.47\% against 3.40\%; exact
Mann--Whitney $p = 0.008$ for each). No unseen-tool audio entered training: the gain
comes from a broader notion of genuine speech.

\textbf{An outside baseline.} AASIST~\cite{jung2022aasist}, scored with its published
weights, rejects 82.3\% of genuine Hindi--English speech, the same failure as S1. Fine-tuned
on our 248 speakers it gains as much as the adapter and is better on Tortoise (CM04
16.7\% against 24.5\%) but worse on the seen tool (1.8\% against 0.9\% on new
speakers), RVC (13.5\% against 6.5\%) and false alarms (2.3\% against 0.5\%): the
voices matter more than the architecture.

\textbf{The final recipe.} Training on 248 speakers in both telephone codecs (simulated
G.711 and real AMR-NB) beats the deployed adapter on ten of eleven sets (in every one of
five seeds on nine of them): new-speaker Tortoise 8.8\% against 30.8\% over the simulated line and 6.0\%
against 36.3\% over AMR-NB. Voice-conversion error rises (8.5\% against 6.1\%), its
shortcut floor is not yet measured, and Chatterbox, a third unseen tool, stays at 32.0\%
(one seed).

\textbf{A larger encoder.} SSL-AASIST~\cite{tak2022wav2vec} (XLS-R 300M, about three times
our encoder) rejects 73.1\% of genuine Hindi--English speech zero-shot. Trained on the
final-recipe data it is better than the adapter on ten of twelve sets over three seeds:
new-speaker Tortoise over AMR-NB 0.55\% against 6.04\%, Chatterbox 11.85\% against 31.98\%,
CM04 1.33\% against 17.73\%. IndicF5 stays at 16.5--21.6\% for both, and we have not measured
its cost on a live call or a shortcut floor for either system on the final data.


\section{Conclusion}

An English-trained detector fails on Hindi--English telephone speech by rejecting
genuine speakers, and a language-controlled test shows the cause is the recording
domain rather than code-mixing. A 1\% low-rank adapter trained on in-domain speech
closes the gap on unseen speakers and keeps English. Generalising to unseen
generators over a telephone line remains open, and we report it against matched
floors rather than against zero. Code, manifests, configurations and per-clip scores
are released; the cloned audio is not.
