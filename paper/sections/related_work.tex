\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Benchmarks and the generalisation question}

ASVspoof 2019 LA~\cite{todisco2019asvspoof} is the reference benchmark for logical
access attacks and the training source for our Stage-1 system. It is English, read
in studio conditions, and its attacks are the text-to-speech and voice conversion
systems of its era. ASVspoof 2021~\cite{liu2023asvspoof2021} added transmission over
telephony codecs to its logical access track and compressed audio to a deepfake
track, and ADD 2022~\cite{yi2022add} moved the task to low-quality and partially fake
Mandarin audio. Detectors trained on ASVspoof degrade sharply on audio collected in
the wild~\cite{muller2022generalize}. That pattern is what makes the question in this
paper worth asking: a field that tunes against one language in one recording
condition can mistake in-domain accuracy for detection ability.

\subsection{Detectors and self-supervised front-ends}

End-to-end countermeasures such as RawNet2~\cite{tak2021rawnet2} and the
graph-attention AASIST~\cite{jung2022aasist} learn from the raw waveform. The current
recipe instead fine-tunes a self-supervised speech encoder: wav2vec~2.0~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec},
its cross-lingual variants XLSR-53~\cite{conneau2021xlsr} and XLS-R~\cite{babu2022xlsr},
or WavLM~\cite{chen2022wavlm}. Used as a front-end with RawBoost waveform
augmentation~\cite{tak2022rawboost}, wav2vec~2.0 gives large gains over hand-designed
features~\cite{tak2022wav2vec}, and reading several encoder layers rather than the
last one helps further~\cite{zhang2024sls}. We adopt the single-encoder form: a
wav2vec~2.0 encoder with attentive statistics pooling~\cite{okabe2018attentive} and a
small classifier.

What this literature does not settle is what the encoder's transfer depends on.
Layer-wise studies of wav2vec~2.0 find linguistic content in the middle and upper
layers~\cite{pasad2021layerwise}; our probes (Section~\ref{sec:probe}) find the
spoofing decision complete by the third, before that content forms. This is
consistent with a detector that reads low-level synthesis artefacts, and with a
change of channel hurting it more than a change of language.

\subsection{Channel and codec robustness}

Telephony degrades detectors, and the usual remedy is augmentation: $\mu$-law and
A-law companding applied to training audio~\cite{das2021companding}, or landline,
cellular and VoIP codecs sampled during training~\cite{chen2021channel}. Closer to
our setting, Tsutsumi et al.~\cite{tsutsumi2026channel} score countermeasures on real
phone and VoIP recordings, find them near chance under the channel mismatch, and
recover by adapting to in-domain degraded speech, and Delgado et
al.~\cite{delgado2026presentation} argue that detectors fail in deployment because
training audio never passes through the presentation channel. RTCFake~\cite{xue2026rtcfake}
passes speech deepfakes through real-time communication platforms such as Zoom and trains
for invariance across them; it targets the processing of conferencing applications, where we
target narrowband telephone codecs. Like Tsutsumi et al.\ and Delgado et al., we train in
the target channel, rendering training and test audio through the same narrowband
G.711~\cite{itu1988g711} chain; what we add is a generator held out of training and
evaluated inside that channel, and a shortcut floor computed on the same channel.

\subsection{Parameter-efficient adaptation}

LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} adapts a frozen model through low-rank updates. It has been
applied to wav2vec~2.0 fake-audio detection, where it matches full fine-tuning with
far fewer trainable parameters~\cite{wang2023lora}, and meta-learned to generalise
across attacks~\cite{laakkonen2026metalora}; adapters~\cite{wu2024adapter} and prompt
tuning~\cite{oiso2024prompt} serve the same purpose inside self-supervised front-ends,
the latter for test-time domain adaptation. We use LoRA for a different reason. With
1.13\% of the parameters trainable, the deployed adapter gains code-mixed performance
\emph{and keeps} clean English, whereas fine-tuning the whole encoder on identical data,
from the self-supervised checkpoint, reaches a lower seen-tool error but is at chance on
English, on which it never trains (Section~\ref{sec:main}). We measure adaptation against that native ceiling rather
than only against its starting point.

\subsection{Multilingual and code-switched detection}

Multilingual spoofing corpora now exist: MLAAD~\cite{muller2024mlaad} and
SpeechFake~\cite{huang2025speechfake} span many languages, IndicSynth~\cite{sharma2025indicsynth}
covers twelve Indian languages, and Indic-CodecFake~\cite{girish2026indiccodecfake}
shows English-trained detectors failing on neural-codec fakes in Indic languages, as
SEA-CF~\cite{phukan2026seacf} does for South-East Asian languages. These corpora hold one
language per utterance. Switching inside an utterance has been studied for Arabic--English
in an audio-visual setting~\cite{kuckreja2026habibi}, and Vietnamese--English
code-switching makes up nearly half of the VietPrism corpus~\cite{hoang2026vietprism}, on
which multilingual detectors prove brittle zero-shot. We found no detection study of
Hindi--English code-mixing, in audio, evaluated over a telephone channel.

\subsection{The attacks and the speech}

We generate attacks rather than reuse a fixed attack set, because the code-mixed
attacks we need do not exist as a benchmark. XTTS-v2~\cite{casanova2024xtts} provides
zero-shot cross-lingual cloning from a few seconds of reference audio;
RVC~\cite{rvc2023} provides voice conversion, which changes speaker identity while
preserving the original delivery and therefore probes a different cue;
Tortoise-TTS~\cite{betker2023tortoise} is held out entirely and used only as an
unseen tool at evaluation. AffectDF~\cite{affectdf2026} informs our attack taxonomy,
our reporting format and the shortcut check we build on. MUCS
2021~\cite{diwan2021mucs} supplies the Hindi--English speech the work is built on.

\subsection{How this work differs}

No single ingredient here is new: telephone-channel adaptation, code-switched
fakes, LoRA on a self-supervised detector and held-out generators each have the
precedents above. The contribution is their combination, and the measurement
discipline around it: Hindi--English switching within an utterance, in audio;
training and testing over the same narrowband channel; every result of our systems
compared, by a paired test over speakers, with a shortcut floor fitted on the same training data
and scored on the same clips; adaptation judged by what it keeps of English and
against a native fine-tuning ceiling; and the detector deployed on live calls. We do
not review the speaker verification literature that anti-spoofing was developed to
protect.
