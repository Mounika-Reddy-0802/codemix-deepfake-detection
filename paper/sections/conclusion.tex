\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An English-trained deepfake detector that reaches 0.85\% EER on ASVspoof does not
work on Hindi--English telephone speech. It reaches 48.21\% EER on clean code-mixed
audio and 58.42\% over the channel, with AUC below chance on two of the three channel sets,
and its failure mode is the rejection of genuine speakers rather than the acceptance
of clones. Low-rank adaptation over a frozen encoder, training 1.13\% of the
parameters on speaker-disjoint code-mixed data and its clones, brings channel-matched
error to 2.75\% EER while retaining 2.42\% on clean English. Full fine-tuning from the
self-supervised checkpoint on identical data is better on the tool it saw (0.83\%) and, never
trained on English, at chance there; the channel-trained adapters, built on the English
detector, keep English as well, which is what a deployment needs (the final recipe keeps less of English:
13.44\%, one seed).

The gap is not caused by code-mixing. Holding speaker and recording fixed, the
English-trained detector rejects 183 unseen speakers' genuine English at least as often
as their mixed speech, and the Code-Mixing Index does not predict its score; what it
fails on is the recording domain that code-mixed telephone speech brings with it. The
closure holds on people the model has never heard: on 60 further unseen speakers the
deployed model reaches 9.4\% EER on the seen tool over the channel, with a median
speaker at 2.9\%. One of the two largest gains came from more training speakers: trained on 248
genuine speakers (with seen-tool clones of them) instead of 21, the same recipe is better in every one of five seeds on
new speakers (0.91\% against 10.75\%), on both unseen tools (Tortoise 13.59\% against
29.82\% on new speakers, IndicF5 27.25\% against 35.40\%) and in false alarms (0.47\%
against 3.40\%), with no unseen-tool audio in training. An established detector, AASIST, shows the same
gap zero-shot (82.3\% of genuine speech rejected) and gains as much from the added
voices on most sets (not on IndicF5 or RVC); fine-tuned on them it is better than our
adapter on Tortoise and worse on the seen tool, RVC and false alarms, so on most sets the
voices matter more than the architecture. A larger encoder, SSL-AASIST, is stronger still: trained on the final-recipe data it reaches 0.51\% on new-speaker Tortoise over the real AMR-NB codec (the adapter: 6.04\%) and 12.86\% on Chatterbox (31.98\%, one adapter seed), and over five seeds it is better in every seed on eight of eleven sets. Its cost on a live call is affordable (1.14\,s per 2\,s hop on two CPU threads, 69\,ms on a GPU), so it is the candidate to deploy after the final recipe; on spontaneous speech one of its trainings calls only 5.8\% of genuine clips fake and another 46.7\%, so it is a lead, not a fix. IndicF5 stays at 16--22\% for both encoders.

The detector runs on a live call. It scores four-second windows of the caller's
speech, escalates only when consecutive windows agree, and warns the callee --- never
the caller --- about ten seconds into the speech, at an operating point calibrated on
development calls. On a pilot of 30 real mobile calls the deployed model warns genuine and
cloned callers alike for two speakers of three, while the final recipe spares most genuine
callers and catches five of six clones of the seen tool, for two speakers later in the call
than offline, but none of an unseen one;
rebuilding the call chain on direct
recordings shows that the real mobile codec takes the deployed model below its threshold on average, so the final
recipe is the model the live system should run now.

\subsection{What we could not do}

\textbf{Against an unseen tool, over a telephone line, we cannot show that this system
works.} On clean audio the adapters transfer to Tortoise-TTS at 5.44--12.01\% EER
against matched floors of 29.49--31.43\%, which is learning beyond the shortcut. Over
the channel the deployed model averages 35.59\% across five training seeds against a
matched floor of 37.79\% and beats it in no seed at the speaker level; its
per-speaker error ranges from 6.4\% to 45.5\%, and the published single run
(30.99\%) was a favourable draw. Trained on XTTS alone, S3 is significantly below its
floor there in three of five seeds (19.72\% against 33.64\%) and S2 in one (25.59\%); with
RVC added to training (which also brings 13 more genuine speakers), neither is shown to beat
the floor. A
variant combining random crops, cue augmentation and a layer mix brings the deployed recipe
to 26.63\% over five seeds, but it was chosen after a test score had been seen, its parts
are not separated, and it still does not clear the floor. A second unseen generator confirms the picture
rather than softening it: IndicF5, a recent flow-matching TTS, sits at 30.6\% over the
channel for the deployed model, about where Tortoise does (29.4\% on the new
speakers). We therefore have no conclusive evidence that the
deployed model detects an unseen tool on a real phone line. This is the sharpest
limitation of the work and it constrains every deployment claim made here. The final recipe
(248 speakers, both telephone codecs) lowers these errors a great deal --- new-speaker
Tortoise over the channel from 30.8\% to 8.8\%, and over the real AMR-NB codec from
36.3\% to 6.0\%, in all five seeds --- and it beats its shortcut floor at the speaker
level in every seed, on new-speaker Tortoise over the channel (8.8\% against 36.5\%) as on
every other set. A third unseen tool, Chatterbox, stays at 32.0\% for the adapter (one
seed); the larger encoder lowers it to 12.9\%. The limitation narrows rather than
disappears: three floors (CM04 over either codec, new-speaker Tortoise over AMR-NB) are
above chance and so weak bars, the final
recipe is not yet the model that runs on live calls, and its real-call test is a pilot of
30 calls in the three authors' own voices (Sec.~\ref{sec:real-calls}).

\textbf{New voices speaking freely are rejected.} Every test set except HiACC is read
lecture audio. On 24 new speakers talking spontaneously (HiACC), the deployed model
calls 54.37\% of genuine clips fake, against 0.89\% for the read lectures, and every
speaker is above 10\%. Neither more read voices nor the real codec changes this: single
models of the deployed and 248-speaker recipes call 38.5--54.4\% of that speech fake, and
the final recipe 43.9\%. No operating point avoids it: sweeping the final recipe's live
threshold over 60\,s calls, choosing nothing, every threshold that catches all Tortoise calls
warns at least 38 of 41 genuine spontaneous calls, and the thresholds that warn fewer than
half catch 4 of 18 unseen-tool calls or fewer.
Adaptation taught the model what genuine lecture recordings sound like, not genuine
Hindi--English speech in general.

\textbf{The deployed model is for Hindi--English calls only.} English passed through
the same telephone channel rises to 24.20\% EER, against 6.50\% for the English-only
S1 (Sec.~\ref{sec:tradeoff}); the 2.42\% English figure holds for clean audio. The final
recipe fails English the other way: it rarely rejects a genuine English speaker but lets
most English clones through (13.44\% EER on clean English, one seed).

\textbf{The attacks are ours, and most are not hard.} We made the code-mixed attacks
because no benchmark provides them, so our numbers are against attacks of our own
construction, screened by our own quality checks. The team rated XTTS-v2 clones of the training pool
1.5 out of 5 for sounding human, and a MOS predictor puts it half a point below the
genuine speech of the same speakers. The exception is IndicF5, whose predicted
naturalness is level with genuine speech --- and no system brings it below 15\% EER over
the channel on average. Detector numbers against easy fakes are optimistic.

\textbf{The telephone leg is simulated, and a real codec hurts.} The channel condition
is an 8~kHz G.711 render with additive noise, validated by measurement and by ear. Through
the reference AMR-NB mobile codec instead, the deployed model's error on new speakers
with the seen tool rises from 9.4\% to 23.7\%, while an adapter trained on clean audio
holds better (12.6\%): channel training fitted one chain, not telephone audio in
general. Training on G.711 and AMR-NB together closes most of that loss (new-speaker
XTTS 7.59\% on AMR-NB against 22.37\%, three seeds). Real mobile calls point the same way:
the deployed model warns every replayed call and most genuine live callers, and the final
recipe holds up on the seen tool (Sec.~\ref{sec:real-calls}). Those calls were recorded on a handset and scored offline;
the live system is implemented against a real telephony carrier, but the carrier leg has
not been exercised on live numbers, and that is the one leg not yet demonstrated end to
end.

\textbf{One live-call behaviour is unexplained.} A genuine caller occasionally loses
consecutive windows on a live call that the same clip never loses offline. We tested
the obvious explanation --- that the codec degrades the audio --- by an offline Opus
round trip with a default encoder, and it does not hold: both genuine clips keep every
window. The real-time transport or the browser's own adaptive encoder, which that test
did not reproduce, remain the likeliest causes, and we have not isolated it.

\textbf{Interpretability covers S1 and the deployed S2 only.} S3, the final recipe and
SSL-AASIST were not probed, and the probe set is five calls (with 86 English clips for
the language check): enough to refute an explanation, not to characterise one.

\subsection{Future work}

The first is spontaneous speech, because it decides whether the system can face real
callers: every genuine 60\,s spontaneous call is warned by both the deployed model and the
final recipe (Sec.~\ref{sec:system-calls}). More read voices and a real codec did not move
it, so it needs spontaneous genuine speech in training, from a speaker-disjoint corpus kept
apart from every test set, together with clones made from the same speakers' voices so that
speaking style cannot become the shortcut. Second, English: the final recipe lets most
English clones through, so a mixed-language service needs English telephone audio in
training or a language-identification step that routes English calls to S1.

After those, the most valuable result is the one that would change a deployment decision:
\emph{what makes an unseen tool detectable over a narrowband channel?} A single-clip
observation in our probing suggests a direction --- adaptation may have moved the model's
evidence for genuine speech into the pitch-harmonic band that survives a codec --- so
training explicitly for band-limited evidence, rather than merely training on band-limited
audio, is worth testing. The exploratory variant of Sec.~\ref{sec:variants} (crops,
augmentation and a layer mix together) should be confirmed on a second unseen tool and its
parts separated, and it carries a lesson for model selection: the in-domain dev set
did not register a 9-point gain on the unseen tool, so choosing among detectors needs
a validation split built from a synthesis tool held out from training, separate from
the one used for testing.

Beyond that: running the final recipe in the live system, which the real calls support;
exercising the telephony carrier on live numbers; real development calls, to set a stricter
speech gate for the first seconds of a call and the operating point together, since the false
alarms on genuine real calls start with the quiet before the first word but do not end there;
extending the
corpus beyond one code-mixed pair and one recording domain, since the present gap
measures \emph{English studio to Hindi--English lecture} and not code-mixing in
isolation; and harder attacks, since a detector is only ever as tested as the fakes
it was shown.
