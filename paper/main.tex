\title{Recording Domain, Not Code-Mixing: Closing the Generalisation Gap of
Audio Deepfake Detection on Hindi--English Telephone Speech}
% \author{...}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Audio deepfake detectors report error rates below one percent on English benchmarks.
We ask what that means for Hindi--English code-mixed speech over a telephone line, where
voice fraud happens. A wav2vec~2.0 detector trained on ASVspoof 2019 LA reaches 0.85\%
equal error rate (EER) on English but 58.42\% over a simulated narrowband channel, because
it rejects genuine speakers (94.8\% of genuine Hindi--English speech). A language-controlled
test on 183 unseen speakers shows code-mixing is not the cause: genuine English is rejected
as often as mixed speech. Low-rank adaptation of the encoder (1.13\% of the parameters)
lowers channel-matched error to 2.75\%. The largest further gains come from more genuine
voices and real codec audio: trained on 248 speakers over both simulated G.711 and real
AMR-NB, error on unseen Tortoise clones over AMR-NB falls from 36.3\% to 6.0\% over five
seeds, with no unseen-tool audio in training. A larger encoder (SSL-AASIST) trained the
same way reaches 0.5\% there and 12.9\% on a third unseen generator, Chatterbox, while
IndicF5 stays at 15.8--21.8\%. Both final systems beat a shortcut floor of cheap signal
statistics in every seed on every test set, where the deployed model never did on the
unseen tool over the channel; three of those floors are above chance and so weak bars. Spontaneous speech is still
rejected. The detector runs on live calls and warns the callee about ten seconds into the
speech.
\end{abstract}
